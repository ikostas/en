Educational Videos Are Still Worth It

  • 20 November 2026
  • #opensource,
  • #productivity

Intro

I personally use YouTube largely as an educational resource, and it makes me sad to see some YouTubers abandoning their channels, blaming AI, lower attention spans and structural crises. I'm a fan of educational videos, although it's better to try and hack it yourself, as well as read the manual. What's so special about educational videos?

  • You can find a video that explains the solution the way you like it (or when the author struggles with exactly the same issue you face)
  • It's about trust: you see the solution actually working, you can see all the steps that lead to it, and you start to believe that you can do it too
  • It's asynchronous: you can find the time that suits you best; it may be 2am, when you can watch it, pause or watch again some moments that are important

I'm working in a B2B/IT context, and it's specific for a number of reasons. It's restrictive in terms of software, format or the goals the videos should achieve. At the same time, I find a space for creativity inside these restrictive boundaries, and I'd like to tell you my way of solving this problem: recording a video that actually helps someone.

Preparation

Well, you can start recording right away, but you'll learn quickly that preparation is key. In an IT context, the programs and settings should work, so there's no room for errors or even warnings. The last time I was doing it, it took me 10 tries to set up the data in the ERP system, figure out the right sequence of actions and get comfortable with this logic myself. But I don't rely on my memory alone!

So the next step is to write down the script. You can treat it as a guideline, feeling free to deviate from it, or even use a mind map just for the structure, but I prefer the full text, as it reveals inconsistencies early on. The next step is to read the script out loud, to make sure it sounds good and you are comfortable with it. You'd probably want to read it a few more times to remember the scenario.

There are a few life hacks to calm down if you feel too much pressure or anxiety: meditate and breathe, or close your eyes and play the whole recording session in your head beforehand. I allow my anxiety to kick in this time, so the situation will be familiar to the brain, and it will think, "Oh, I've been there, so nothing happened," and it will calm down.

Recording

I prefer to split the recording sessions into 2-3 minute chunks, to glue them together afterwards (which is a simple operation for the computer, by the way), and you'll be able to go relatively fast through one chunk, then take a break. Some important points of recording operations with software:

  • Try to start exactly where you finished the last chunk; I try even not to move the mouse.
  • Don't split the logical operations that should finish successfully from beginning to end; it will show up and you'll just confuse yourself.
  • You can record several times, but in some cases you'll need to change the data or start from the beginning (regrettably): for instance, your viewers need to see that the production orders are derived from the production plan you worked with before.

The tech side of the effort is the software. If you have an opportunity to install OBS Studio, just go for it. If you are in a restricted Windows environment, surprisingly, you can use the infamous Snipping Tool to record the screencast and the audio, and it works. If you need a video from your camera in the corner, go for OBS Studio. If you prefer to record audio afterwards, I'd not recommend that. It always seemed strange, and you'll need a video editor (one more program).

Making a product

To glue the things together, you'd need ffmpeg, which is a command-line utility available for Mac, Linux and Windows. I used it in WSL for obvious reasons; it works just fine.

The first thing to do is to glue the files together, and ffmpeg does it without re-encoding if the encodings of all the files match. As input, you feed ffmpeg a list of files in a particular order (Captain Obvious here), and you get a single file.

The second step is to compress the video (with ffmpeg as well); I'd just use a chatbot to get the proper command-line parameters. In my case, it compressed a 1.2 GB video to 42 MB, which made it a lot easier to distribute without any quality loss! Again, compressing your video with the right parameters is respecting your viewers. And you never know how many views you'll get!

Conclusion

Nothing new here: preparation is key. And having a plan like "How am I going to edit the video?" helps with the technical issues. I did some video editing; the issue here is that it's a time-consuming and resource-consuming process. Some people enjoy it; you can apply effects and transitions, but it takes about 6x the recording time. I just have some stuff to do. :) If a lack of time or video-editing skills prevents you from sharing information with the world or your colleagues, now you have a recipe, I hope.
